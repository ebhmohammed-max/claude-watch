Contrebandiers Cave sits on the Atlantic coast of Morocco, close enough to the sea that you can hear it. From layers dated between a hundred and twenty thousand and ninety thousand years ago, Emily Hallett and her colleagues recovered more than twelve thousand bone fragments. Sixty-two had been shaped by people. The tool you would walk past is a scraper: the rib of an antelope or a wild cow, split into a flat, spatula-like shape. It looks like nothing. Under a microscope it carries a record — short deep grooves, and a polish from being pulled again and again across the inside of a raw hide. You do not scrape a hide because you want to eat it later. Scraping removes fat, membrane and hair so that skin stops being a corpse and starts being a material. The detail that settles it is on the animals. Hallett found cut marks across the paws and jaws of sand foxes, golden jackals and wildcats — a pattern that does not match butchering for meat. The skin had been opened at the feet and pulled, in one piece, over the head. You do that when the fur is the point. It does not prove anyone there sewed a coat. The researchers call it strong proxy evidence. But it shows an animal chosen for its pelt, and a tool that makes the result soft.
