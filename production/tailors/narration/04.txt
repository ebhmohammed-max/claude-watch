If you want clothing from hides, the difficult step is not cutting. It is joining. You can fold a hide, or tie it with a strip of leather. But for a sleeve, or a seam that holds under strain, you have to pass thread through material — with a tool fine enough not to tear the hole wider than the thread. Awls came first: a sharpened bone, pushed through to open a hole. They work — but they leave a gap, and every puncture weakens the hide. A needle carries the thread with it, so the hole is exactly the width of the thread. The oldest bone needle most often cited comes from Denisova Cave in southern Siberia, around fifty thousand years old. It is about seven centimetres long, made from the bone of a large bird, with an eye worked through the end. It came from layers that also produced Denisovan remains — though it cannot be assigned to a species with confidence. By fifty thousand years ago, someone was drilling a hole through a sliver of bone without splitting it. That is not a crude object. That is a specialist. And then something odd. Eyed needles do not appear everywhere at once. They show up in the cold parts of Eurasia from around forty thousand years ago, as the climate tips toward the last glacial maximum. People already had awls. People already had clothes. So why invent a harder tool?
