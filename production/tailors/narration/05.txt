In 2024, Ian Gilligan and colleagues argued that the answer is not survival in the simple sense. A scraped hide gives you a loose garment. Enough for a cool evening. Not enough for a glacial winter, where what keeps you alive is not one thick layer but several thin ones — and the layer closest to the skin is the one that has to fit. That needs fine sewing. Fine sewing needs an eyed needle. In Gilligan's phrasing, the innovation comes down to making underwear. People decorate themselves when skin is visible: ochre, scars, paint, beads pressed to the body. Cover the body completely, for months, because staying warm is life and death, and all that signalling has nowhere to go. So it moves onto the clothing. Eyed needles attach beads and shells and strips of fur to the outside of a garment, fine enough to read from a distance. Clothing stops being thermal and becomes social. It is a hypothesis, built from timing and tool, and Gilligan says so. But once you look for it, the record is full of it.
