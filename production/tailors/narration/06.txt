In the foothills of the Caucasus, in the republic of Georgia, there is a cave called Dzudzuana. In 2009, a team led by Eliso Kvavadze and Ofer Bar-Yosef was not looking for clothing. They were studying ancient climate, which meant pollen, and counting grains under a microscope. What they found instead were fibres. Wild flax. Thousands of them, in layers up to thirty-four thousand years old. Some were twisted — spun, deliberately, into cord. Some were knotted. And some were coloured: black, grey, turquoise, and one that was pink. The colours came from plants. Someone had taken a plant, extracted fibre, spun it into thread, and dyed it — for cord, for baskets, for sewing, or for all three. No textile survives from Dzudzuana. What survives is fibre, at a scale you need a microscope to see. Dyeing is not a survival technology. You do not colour a rope because the rope works better. Around the same period, at Pavlov in the Czech Republic, fired clay preserved the impression of woven fibre — a textile that no longer exists. So it is not hides versus cloth, with cloth arriving late. It is both, for a very long time, in the same toolkits.
