Two hundred kilometres east of Moscow, near the town of Vladimir, is a site called Sunghir. Around thirty-four thousand years ago, people were buried there with extraordinary care. The best known grave holds a man between roughly thirty-five and forty-five years old. He was covered in red ochre, and he was covered in beads. About three thousand of them. Mammoth ivory, each one carved, each one pierced, each one small enough to be a problem for a modern jeweller. Alongside them: armbands of ivory, and hundreds of pierced fox teeth. Then those beads were attached to what he was wearing — they were found lying in rows, in the pattern of a garment, as if sewn onto it. That is the moment clothing stops being about weather. A hide keeps you warm. A hide carrying three thousand hand-cut beads announces who this person was, what their community could spend, and what they believed the dead deserved. Nearby, two children were buried head to head, with ivory spears and discs and thousands more beads. Whatever the beads meant, they were not a privilege of age.
